How a logit gets fit

Likelihood Explorer

"Fit by maximum likelihood" sounds like magic. It's a search for the parameter values that make the data you actually saw as probable as possible, and the shape of the peak tells you how sure to be.

1A coin

You flip a coin 20 times and get 14 heads. For any guess p of the heads probability, the chance of exactly this data is ph(1−p)n−h. Its logarithm, the log-likelihood, is easier to work with: ℓ(p) = h·ln p + (n−h)·ln(1−p). Drag p and find the top.

The peak is always at p̂ = h/n, the share of heads. More interesting is how sharp the peak is. Slide n up while keeping the same share of heads, and the curve narrows. The curvature at the top, ℓ''(p̂) = −n / (p̂(1−p̂)), measures how fast the likelihood falls off, and SE = 1/√(−ℓ''(p̂)) = √(p̂(1−p̂)/n). That's the familiar standard error of a proportion, recovered from geometry.

2Two coefficients: a surface

A logistic regression has a log-likelihood too: ℓ(β₀, β₁) = Σ [yᵢ ln Pᵢ + (1−yᵢ) ln(1−Pᵢ)] with Pᵢ = 1/(1+e−(β₀+β₁xᵢ)). Below, 80 students' study hours (x) and whether they passed (y). With two coefficients the log-likelihood is a hill over the (β₀, β₁) plane. Click anywhere on the map to start the optimizer there, then step through.

Newton–Raphson (what HW4 2B used) treats the hill as a bowl. It reads the slope (gradient g) and the curvature (Hessian H) where it stands and jumps to the top of that bowl: β ← β − H⁻¹g. Near the peak the bowl is nearly exact, so it lands in about 5 steps. Gradient ascent only follows the slope. On this tilted, stretched hill (β₀ and β₁ trade off against each other) it zig-zags for hundreds of steps, and too large a learning rate makes it overshoot and diverge.

3Curvature → standard errors

At the peak, the Hessian holds the curvature in every direction. Flip its sign and invert it, and you get the covariance matrix of the estimates. The square roots of its diagonal are the standard errors, and the off-diagonal says how the two estimates move together. The 95% ellipse on the map is where ℓ has dropped by 3.0 from the peak (half the 95% χ² value with 2 degrees of freedom).

4Fit it yourself: the fare-bundle conjoint

HW4 2B fit a logit to 1,500 choice screens (each: two fare bundles plus "Not now") with four coefficients. Try to find them by hand: move the sliders to make the log-likelihood as high as you can. Then let Newton–Raphson do it.

Tip: start with price (it has the biggest effect per unit), then the constant, then bag and flex. When every slider is at its best given the others, you're at the peak, and the SEs come from how sharply ℓ falls if you nudge any of them.
